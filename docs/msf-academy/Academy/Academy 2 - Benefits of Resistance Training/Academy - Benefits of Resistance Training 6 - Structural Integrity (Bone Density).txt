Benefits of Resistance Training
Structural Integrity - Bone Density
Bone density improvements from strength training are primarily local, but there is also a meaningful systemic influence. Both matter, and they work together.
Local effects dominate
Bone adapts most strongly at the specific sites that experience mechanical loading. When a muscle contracts forcefully, it pulls on the bone at its attachment points. This localized stress stimulates osteocytes in that region, which signals osteoblasts to build or preserve bone. For example, strength training that loads the hips and thighs is especially effective for improving or maintaining bone density in the femoral neck and proximal femur, which are critical fracture sites.
This is why targeted resistance training is more effective for bone health than general activity like walking. Bones respond to the magnitude and direction of loading at specific joints rather than overall movement or calorie expenditure.
Systemic effects also contribute
Strength training also creates systemic influences through hormonal responses and muscle-derived signaling molecules. Myokines released during muscle contraction circulate throughout the body and support bone metabolism more broadly. These signals help regulate bone remodeling, reduce bone breakdown, and support the activity of bone-forming cells.
Additionally, strength training improves muscle mass, coordination, and balance across the body, which lowers fall risk systemically. While this does not directly increase bone density everywhere, it significantly reduces fracture risk overall.
What this means in practice
The greatest improvements in bone density occur at the bones and joints that are directly loaded during training. Systemic effects support bone health throughout the body but do not replace the need to load vulnerable sites directly. For individuals with osteoporosis or osteopenia, this is why a well-designed strength program includes exercises that safely load the hips, spine, and major joints rather than relying on general exercise alone.
In summary, bone adaptations are mostly local, supported by systemic signaling. The best outcomes come from consistent, targeted strength training applied with appropriate intensity, safety, and control.
More on the topic of Myokines and Bone Density
Strength training plays a meaningful role in improving bone health in individuals with osteopenia and osteoporosis, not only through mechanical loading of bone but also through the release of muscle-derived signaling molecules known as myokines.
When skeletal muscle contracts during a high intensity effort, it releases myokines into circulation. These myokines act as endocrine and paracrine messengers that influence bone remodeling by promoting bone formation and inhibiting bone resorption. This biochemical signaling works alongside mechanical stress to improve bone density and structural integrity.
From a mechanical standpoint, high-effort strength training exposes bones to loading forces that stimulate osteocytes, the bone’s primary mechanosensors. This stimulation activates osteoblasts, the cells responsible for building bone. Importantly, bones respond best to higher-magnitude loading rather than repetitive low-force activity, making properly supervised strength training particularly effective for bone preservation and improvement.
Key myokines involved in bone metabolism include:
Irisin, which is released during muscle contraction and has been shown to stimulate osteoblast activity and increase cortical bone mass.
Myostatin, which normally inhibits muscle growth; resistance training reduces myostatin levels, indirectly supporting both muscle and bone development.
Interleukin-6 (IL-6), when released transiently during exercise, promotes bone turnover in a favorable way by supporting osteoblast differentiation.
Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), produced locally in muscle and bone, enhances bone formation and mineralization.
For individuals with osteopenia or osteoporosis, increasing muscle strength also improves balance, joint stability, and coordination, reducing fall risk, which is a critical factor in fracture prevention. Additionally, the improved neuromuscular confidence that comes from structured strength training supports long-term adherence, which is essential for maintaining bone adaptations.
In summary, strength training enhances bone density through a dual mechanism: direct mechanical loading of the skeletal system and indirect biochemical signaling via myokines. This makes it one of the most effective non-pharmacological strategies for managing and improving bone health in populations at risk for bone loss, when applied progressively and safely.

Bone Density “talking points”
Strength training helps improve bone health in two important ways, which is especially beneficial for people with osteopenia or osteoporosis.
First, bones get stronger when they are exposed to appropriate stress. When muscles work hard against resistance, they pull on the bones they attach to. This signals the body that the bones need to become stronger to handle that demand. Over time, this can slow bone loss and, in many cases, improve bone density. Importantly, bones respond best to meaningful effort rather than light, repetitive activity, which is why properly supervised strength training is so effective.
Second, working muscles release helpful substances into the body during strength training. These substances send signals that support bone building and repair. In simple terms, strong muscles help communicate to your bones that they need to stay strong as well. This internal signaling works alongside the physical stress of exercise to support healthier bones.
Strength training also improves muscle strength, balance, and joint stability. This reduces the risk of falls, which is one of the biggest concerns for people with low bone density. Feeling stronger and more stable in everyday movement is just as important as changes that happen inside the bones themselves.
At MaxStrength Fitness, the goal is not to overload the body but to apply the right amount of resistance in a controlled and safe way. Exercises are tailored to your abilities, performed with careful attention to form, and progressed gradually over time. This approach allows your muscles and bones to adapt safely while minimizing risk.
In short, strength training helps your bones by giving them a reason to stay strong and by improving the support system around them. When done correctly, it is one of the most effective and practical tools for protecting bone health and maintaining independence as you age.
