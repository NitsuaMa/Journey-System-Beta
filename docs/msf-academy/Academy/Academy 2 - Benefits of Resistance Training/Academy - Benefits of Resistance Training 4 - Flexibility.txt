Benefits of Resistance Training
Flexibility
Flexibility is improvable through static stretching in the form of increased stretch tolerance.  Flexibility on its own, however, is not a productive factor in human movement; it simply permits muscular work through a range of motion.
Participants in activities such as dance, martial arts, and gymnastics may benefit from a specific supplementary stretching program; however, a healthy level of flexibility can be achieved and maintained with resistance training alone.  Performing resistance exercise over an optimal range of motion, from a contracted, or shortened muscular position, to a stretched, or lengthened muscular position, ensures that the flexibility and mobility of the involved joints are stimulated to improve.
Although not all exercises provide an extreme degree of stretch, the range of motion over which most exercises are performed greatly exceeds that which the involved joints move through during normal daily activities.  Resistance training performed over a full range of motion provides an optimized level of flexibility needed for the majority of physical activities.
As an aside, it seems that there is a pervasive over-valuing of flexibility in today’s culture where it has become desirable to be hyper flexible.  Not only is this beyond what most people would need, it can actually be detrimental as it can lead to a number of well documented risks including:
Joint instability: Too much range of motion (ROM) reduces passive stability from ligaments and joint capsules, increasing the risk of sprains, subluxations, and dislocations.
Altered neuromuscular control: Because the passive structures don’t restrict movement, muscles must compensate to stabilize joints. This can lead to fatigue, inefficient mechanics, and overuse injuries.
Soft tissue injuries: Hypermobility is associated with higher rates of muscle strains, tendinopathies, and ligament tears.
Chronic pain: People with generalized joint hypermobility often report musculoskeletal pain, even in the absence of acute injury, likely due to micro-instability and altered load distribution.
Early onset osteoarthritis: Repeated joint instability and abnormal loading patterns may accelerate degenerative changes.
Functional performance limitations: While flexibility is beneficial up to a point, “too much” often reduces strength, power production, and joint integrity—particularly in high-force, high-speed activities.
This current understanding is supported by a 2023 meta-analysis published in the Journal of Sports Medicine comparing 55 studies on the topic of flexibility gained from strength training vs. dedicated stretching.  The results indicated that resistance training improves flexibility to the same extent as stretching.  And of even greater interest is the fact that these adaptations are not received from bodyweight exercises alone.  In order to receive the flexibility benefits from resistance training, an external load is required.[¹]
While we are not opposed to static stretching, we do not believe it is a necessary or productive component for our Continuous Tension protocol.  For those subjects that have a chronically “tight” area, static stretching may provide some relief assuming the stretches are performed safely and effectively.  But as for general flexibility and mobility, a properly performed strength training program like ours will improve those qualities through consistent application.
