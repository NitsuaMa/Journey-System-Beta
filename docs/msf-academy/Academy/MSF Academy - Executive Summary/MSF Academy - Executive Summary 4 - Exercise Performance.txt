Exercise Performance
Exercise Induced Headaches
Before we proceed further into the topic of Exercise Performance, it is important to bring awareness to q21Exercise Induced Headaches (EIH) are a rare but important phenomenon observed in high-intensity strength training environments, including those utilizing the MaxStrength Fitness Continuous Tension protocol. While uncommon, their potential occurrence requires awareness, education, and consistent handling to preserve client safety and confidence in the method.
An EIH is defined as a headache that is caused or aggravated by exertion during strength training. The presentation often begins as mild stiffness or dull pain in the back of the head and can intensify if exercise continues, sometimes radiating over the head and into the orbital region of one eye. The prevailing theory suggests EIHs are related to impaired venous return during high effort, leading to increased pressure in emissary veins of the skull.
When and Why EIHs May Occur
EIHs are most often observed under specific conditions rather than randomly. Franchise owners should understand these patterns to better recognize appropriate trainer responses.
Most commonly reported during the first few workouts as a client adapts to high-intensity effort
May occur later in training cycles as intensity of effort progressively increases
Often associated with breath holding or excessive Valsalva maneuver during exertion
Most frequently linked to high systemic-demand exercises, particularly the Leg Press
Importantly, EIHs do not cause long-term injury or structural damage when handled correctly.
Prevention Through Client Education
Prevention is the most effective strategy for managing EIHs within the MSF system. Clients must be informed during the consultation process that EIHs are rare but possible and that early communication is critical.
Clients are instructed to report any pre-existing headache before training begins
Clients must immediately communicate any onset of head discomfort during exercise
Trainers rely on client feedback, as EIH symptoms are not externally observable
This shared responsibility between trainer and client is a foundational safety principle within the MSF methodology.
Immediate Response and Safety Protocol
When any head discomfort is reported or sensed, exercise termination is mandatory.
The load must be safely and immediately unloaded
The client should remain seated or positioned comfortably
Feet and legs remain below heart level
Eyes closed, head supported and still, with minimal sensory input
Speaking is discouraged during the initial recovery period
A darkened environment may be helpful if light sensitivity is present
The trainer remains with the client throughout the recovery period
The workout may only continue if the client is completely unable to imagine the head pain. If any sensation remains, the session must be terminated.
Redefining Failure and Exercise Progression
Following an EIH, the definition of momentary muscular failure must be adjusted.
Failure is no longer defined by muscular limitation
Failure becomes the first perception or imagination of head discomfort
Subsequent workouts adhere strictly to this redefined failure point
This redefinition allows continued training while prioritizing neurological and vascular safety.
Modifying the Exercise Routine
Longer-term management focuses on reducing exposure to triggering conditions while maintaining overall program integrity.
The triggering exercise is removed temporarily from the routine
When reintroduced, it is performed last in the session
Intensity is reduced through lower load and/or fewer repetitions
Emphasis is placed on continuous, unrestricted breathing throughout the set
If the same exercise triggers a second EIH, it should be removed for the foreseeable future, and all exercises should be progressed more conservatively.
Tracking, Pattern Recognition, and Gradual Reintroduction
Accurate record keeping is essential to successful resolution.
Trainers document the exact exercise, repetition range, and intensity level associated with the EIH
Patterns are identified to establish safe repetition ceilings
Progression occurs gradually and systematically over multiple weeks
In some cases, beginning the session with Cervical Extension may be beneficial to promote blood flow through the neck and upper back and to pre-fatigue the cervical musculature.
With consistent tracking, conservative progression, and strict adherence to communication and termination protocols, clients can typically return to completing the full routine without recurrence of EIH symptoms.
Summary
EIH management within MSF is not about eliminating intensity, but about intelligently controlling it. Proper education, immediate response, and disciplined progression ensure that the Continuous Tension protocol remains both effective and safe when applied at high levels of effort.
Components of a Repetition
Importance of the Individual Repetition
Within the MaxStrength Fitness methodology, the individual repetition represents the smallest and most important unit of measurement in a workout. The quality of each repetition directly determines the quality of the workout itself, and over time, the cumulative effect of thousands of repetitions determines long-term client success.
For this reason, mastering repetition execution is a primary objective of the MSF protocol. Progress at MSF is not defined solely by increased load or repetitions, but by the ability to perform each repetition with greater precision, control, and consistency over time. Training better occurs on a rep-to-rep basis and reflects a continual refinement of technique, intent, and awareness.
The Phases of a Repetition
Each repetition within the MSF system can be understood as a continuous cycle consisting of five distinct phases. While these phases are described separately for clarity, they function as an integrated whole during proper execution.
Exercise initiation through either a gradual load up or a handoff
Concentric phase
Upper turnaround
Eccentric phase
Lower turnaround
Understanding and controlling each phase is essential to safe and effective high-intensity training.
Exercise Initiation: Gradual Load Up or Handoff
Every exercise begins in one of two ways within the MSF methodology: a gradual load up or a handoff. These are the only acceptable methods of initiating an exercise and must always occur under direct instructor guidance.
The gradual load up, along with the lower turnaround, is among the most critical components of the entire protocol. Proper initiation ensures appropriate loading, minimizes unnecessary acceleration, and establishes correct movement control from the very first repetition. Clients must be held accountable for correct initiation, but mastery must first exist at the instructor level in order to be taught and enforced consistently.
Concentric and Eccentric Phases
The concentric phase refers to the lifting portion of the movement, during which the target muscles contract and shorten as the joints move from the start position toward the end of the range of motion.
The eccentric phase refers to the lowering portion of the movement, during which the target muscles lengthen while controlling the load as the joints return toward the starting position.
While repetition speed is important, MSF places greater emphasis on how force is managed throughout these phases rather than on speed alone.
Turnarounds and Direction Changes
The upper and lower turnarounds represent the moments of direction change between concentric and eccentric phases. These transitions are the points at which the potential for acceleration, momentum, and excessive force is greatest.
Turnaround performance is more critical to safety and effective muscular loading than repetition speed alone. Mastery of turnarounds is considered the single most important attribute of properly performed exercise within the MSF system.
Lower Turnaround Execution
For most exercises, the lower turnaround is performed using a perpetual touch-and-go motion. In this approach, the lifted plates lightly contact the remaining plates on the weight stack before immediately reversing direction. This minimizes momentum while maintaining continuous tension.
An important exception exists for compound pulling exercises where the machine’s range of motion exceeds the involved joint’s range of motion. In these cases, joint extension may be reached before the plates touch, and instructors must account for this difference.
The technical skill and attention required to execute a proper lower turnaround are often underestimated by clients and even some trainers. However, along with the gradual load up, the lower turnaround forms the foundation of safe and effective MSF exercise execution and must be persistently taught and enforced.
Upper Turnaround Techniques
Upper turnaround execution varies depending on the type of exercise being performed.
Some exercises use a continuous pass-through technique, similar in nature to the lower turnaround
Other exercises require a squeeze technique, involving a brief increase in effort in the fully contracted position before lowering the load
Single-joint movements and compound pulling exercises most commonly utilize a squeeze technique.
Importantly, instructors must provide clear guidance on how to perform a squeeze. Simply instructing a client to “squeeze” is insufficient and often leads to incorrect behaviors such as clenching the hands rather than increasing muscular effort in the target muscles.
For example, during a leg extension, effective instruction may include cues such as continuing to straighten the knee or attempting to straighten it further in the contracted position. Although no additional movement occurs, the intent to continue the action increases quadriceps contraction and achieves the desired training effect.
Continuous Improvement Through Precision
Attention to repetition details such as initiation, turnarounds, and squeeze execution reflects the MSF commitment to continuous improvement. These refinements are not optional but represent the ongoing pursuit of better execution over time. This mindset of incremental improvement, or Kaizen, is central to the development of both clients and MSF Exercise Practitioners.
Summary
At MaxStrength Fitness, long-term success is built on the consistent execution of high-quality repetitions. Each repetition is a controlled cycle of initiation, movement, and turnarounds, with particular emphasis placed on gradual load ups and precise direction changes. By prioritizing repetition quality over speed or load alone, and by continually refining execution through attention to detail, the MSF methodology delivers safe, effective, and sustainable high-intensity strength training.
Gradual Load Up
Role of the Gradual Load Up
The gradual load up is a foundational component of the MaxStrength Fitness Continuous Tension protocol and applies to all compound pushing movements and most single-joint exercises. In these movements, the exercise begins from a dead stop in the stretched position, making the initiation of force a critical moment for both safety and effectiveness.
The objective of the gradual load up is not simply to move the weight, but to introduce mechanical tension to the target musculature in a controlled and deliberate manner. How the load is first engaged sets the tone for the entire repetition and, by extension, the quality of the set.
Common Errors at Exercise Initiation
Most trainees instinctively attempt to initiate movement with a sudden exertion, often accompanied by breath holding through a Valsalva maneuver. This reaction typically occurs because the body is in a biomechanically disadvantaged position at the start of the movement.
Two common patterns are frequently observed:
A forceful initial push to ensure the weight moves
An abrupt push followed by immediate cessation when the weight does not move right away, leading the client to believe they cannot lift the load
In both cases, the client has shifted focus away from the true objective of resistance training, which is to create high levels of mechanical tension within the targeted muscle fibers rather than simply producing movement.
Mechanical Tension as the Primary Objective
Within the MSF framework, the purpose of resistance training is to recruit as many motor units as possible through sustained mechanical tension. When force is applied suddenly, momentum is introduced, and meaningful tension is reduced at the most critical portion of the movement.
The trainer must consistently remind and re-teach clients how to initiate exercises properly from a dead stop. Assuming appropriate load selection, correct positioning, and adequate bracing, force must be applied gradually while maintaining continuous, unforced breathing.
Executing the Gradual Load Up
A proper gradual load up requires the client to increase effort incrementally until movement begins. The client should be coached to “go through their gears,” increasing force slowly in search of the minimum amount of effort required to initiate movement.
This process may take several seconds, depending on the magnitude of the load. During this time:
Force increases progressively rather than abruptly
Breathing remains continuous and relaxed
No expectation of immediate movement exists
This approach improves safety and effectiveness by allowing tension to build within the muscle before movement occurs.
Controlling Progressive Overload
Mastery of the gradual load up allows clients to create higher levels of muscular tension with less external load. This reduces unnecessary risk while improving training stimulus.
If force is applied suddenly while holding the breath, movement becomes easier, which can falsely suggest that a load increase is warranted. Premature load progression often results in deteriorating form, loss of control, and excessive loading. By controlling the load up, trainers manage an important variable in the progression of intensity.
Practical Example: Leg Extension
The leg extension provides a clear illustration of the importance of a gradual load up. Without careful control, the first portion of the movement often occurs almost instantly, creating momentum and diminishing muscular tension.
Proper execution requires the client to understand that movement will not occur immediately. As force is gradually increased, slack within both the machine and the body must be eliminated before the weight begins to move. This includes compression of pads, tightening of cables or belts, and stabilization of the body against the seat.
Movement begins only once this slack has been fully taken out. This deliberate process reinforces tension and minimizes momentum at the most vulnerable point in the range of motion.
Tension at Initiation
The gradual load up typically occurs over a minimum of three seconds, and may take upwards of five seconds or longer in exercises involving larger loads, such as the leg press. Regardless of duration, the defining characteristic is a smooth and incremental buildup of force rather than a sudden exertion.
The result is a gradual introduction of mechanical tension to the target muscles, consistent with the principles of the MSF Continuous Tension protocol.
Summary
The gradual load up is a critical skill that directly influences both the safety and effectiveness of MSF training. By emphasizing a slow, incremental application of force from a dead stop, trainers help clients maximize mechanical tension, reduce unnecessary loading, and maintain control at the most demanding portion of the movement. Consistent reinforcement of this concept ensures high-quality repetition execution and supports long-term progress within the MaxStrength Fitness system.
Range of Motion
Individualized and Constantly Evaluated
In the MSF methodology, every exercise is performed through a range of motion that is both safe and effective for the individual client. This range is specific to the exercise and the client and must be continuously monitored and adjusted by the instructor, even for advanced clients who are familiar with the equipment.
Exercise Practitioners are always seeking ways to optimize the client experience by ensuring movement is controlled, precise, and aligned with the principles of the MSF Continuous Tension protocol.
Safe Range of Motion
Safe range of motion refers to the distance over which muscles and joints are not exposed to potentially harmful forces and the client does not experience pain due to injury or physical limitations.
Example: On the seated leg curl, the start position and lower turnaround are set to avoid hyperextension of the knee
If a client’s range is limited by injury, pain, or flexibility, the machine’s range should be adjusted accordingly
These adjustments must be clearly documented in the client’s chart and updated as needed
Safe range of motion protects joint integrity and ensures clients can train without risk of harm, maintaining confidence and consistency.
Effective Range of Motion
Effective range of motion refers to the portion of movement in which the target muscles experience meaningful resistance.
Example: On the leg press, frontal thigh muscles may unload as the knee approaches full extension; pushing beyond this point offers minimal tension and places unnecessary stress on the joint
By contrast, on the leg extension, the quadriceps are under high tension as the knee straightens fully, and the movement should maximize contraction for effective stimulus
Exercise selection, machine adjustments, and client coaching must prioritize the muscles experiencing true mechanical tension, rather than simply moving through a full anatomical range.
Continuous Adjustment and Practitioner Expertise
Range of motion may need to be modified at any moment depending on the client’s condition, flexibility, and strength level.
Instructors must be proactive in optimizing every repetition for safety, efficacy, and client comfort
Mastery of this skill is an essential part of developing as an MSF Exercise Practitioner and reflects the ongoing pursuit of excellence in training delivery
Summary
Range of motion in MSF is both safe and effective, tailored to the individual client, and continually evaluated by the instructor. Safe range protects joints and prevents pain, while effective range ensures meaningful tension on the target muscles. Adjustments must be documented and refined over time, highlighting the expertise and vigilance required of an MSF Exercise Practitioner.
Speed of Movement (Pace)
Purpose of Controlled Pace
In the MSF methodology, the speed at which repetitions are performed is designed to maintain smooth reversals between concentric and eccentric phases while ensuring proper posture, alignment, and positioning throughout the exercise. Smooth changes in direction protect the client from potentially harmful increases in force and maintain continuous mechanical tension on the target muscles.
A controlled pace also prevents the instinctive, unsafe tendency to unload the weight abruptly and then force it back up to avoid discomfort. This sudden, unconscious action can reduce tension on the muscle and increase the risk of injury.
Recommended Repetition Cadence
An approximate 12 to 20-second repetition cycle is generally effective for maintaining controlled movement:
Positive (concentric) phase: 6 to 10 seconds
Upper turnaround: brief pause or continuous movement
Negative (eccentric) phase: 6 to 10 seconds
Lower turnaround: always continuous
Adjustments to cadence can be made for exercises with shorter or longer ranges of motion, but consistency is recommended to avoid confusion. A slower pace may be beneficial for novice trainees or clients requiring extra caution due to injury or physical limitations.
Using Verbal Cadence for Instruction
When introducing a client to the protocol or a new exercise, verbal cadence counting can help them experience the feel of controlled movement. Occasional cadence cues can also assist experienced clients who may unintentionally move too fast or too slowly.
Importantly, the speed of movement should match on both the concentric and eccentric phases to ensure uniform application of mechanical tension throughout the repetition.
Pace Governs Effort
While clients maintain a consistent pace, effort naturally increases as fatigue accumulates. As the movement slows due to fatigue, the client must voluntarily increase their effort to maintain the pace. This is how stimulatory mechanical tension is achieved and why resistance training is effective.
Clients may begin moving slowly not because they intend to, but because fatigue limits their speed
Trainers should encourage clients to push harder to maintain pace and sustain tension, cultivating the ability to train at a higher intensity despite discomfort
Focusing on maintaining pace can help clients move beyond adverse feelings that might otherwise cause premature termination of sets.
Advanced Considerations
As clients progress, a slightly faster pace (closer to 6 seconds per phase) may be advantageous:
Easier to control with smoother repetition
Produces less discomfort while still maintaining high tension
Requires practice and competency; not all clients are suited for this faster cadence
A slower pace (closer to 10 seconds) remains appropriate in situations where safety, client tolerance, or technical skill dictates. Client safety always remains the top priority.
Summary
Controlled pace in MSF training ensures smooth, safe reversals between movement phases, preserves continuous mechanical tension, and governs client effort as fatigue accumulates. Verbal cadence and individualized pacing support both novice and advanced clients in maximizing tension safely. The ideal pace varies based on experience, competency, and safety considerations, emphasizing that speed must always serve quality and control, not simply duration.
Turnaround Technique (Change of Direction)
Importance of Proper Turnarounds
Turnaround technique is a critical element of the MSF Continuous Tension protocol. It ensures that target muscles remain under mechanical tension, prevents abrupt unloading, and protects the joints and muscular structures from potentially harmful forces. Proper turnarounds maintain smooth, continuous movement throughout each repetition and are central to safe and effective exercise performance.
Turnaround performance varies based on:
Start and end points of the exercise
Mechanical or anatomical limitations of joints
Leverage factors and individual range of motion
Mastery of both lower and upper turnarounds is essential for all MSF trainees.
Lower Turnaround on Standard Equipment
When the resistance source can be set down at the start (e.g., selectorized machines where plates lightly touch the stack):
Reduce speed approaching the lower turnaround to minimize impact
Allow the lifted weights to touch the stack without unloading the target musculature
Immediately but slowly begin the positive phase, maintaining continuity (“barely touch, barely start,” or “touch and go”)
This approach applies to compound pushing movements (leg press, chest press, overhead press) and most single-joint or rotary exercises (leg extension, leg curl, lateral raise).
Lower Turnaround When Joint Structures Limit Movement
If the equipment range exceeds the trainee’s joint range:
Slow as the involved joint approaches the end of its tolerable motion
Begin the positive phase immediately but gradually
Example: In a compound row or pulldown, the elbow may straighten before the weights bottom out, defining the lower turnaround at the joint rather than the machine.
When Flexibility Limits Range of Motion
When the negative movement is restricted by muscular flexibility:
Reduce speed approaching the start point
Enter a moderate, safe stretch
Begin the positive phase gradually
Example: A valgus elbow that cannot fully extend or a knee that cannot fully straighten on a leg extension. Reduced range is acceptable in an effort to ensure safety and continuous loading.
Upper Turnaround When Equipment Limits Positive Movement
For exercises with mechanical end stops:
Slow as the movement arm approaches the endpoint to reduce impact
Barely touch or “pass through” the upper turnaround
Begin the negative phase immediately and continuously
Upper Turnaround for Contracted Position
For compound pulling movements or single-joint exercises with meaningful resistance at full contraction:
Slow as the arm approaches the contracted position
Pause briefly (1–2 seconds) to maintain control
Gradually ease out of contraction and begin the negative
From the third repetition onward, employ the squeeze technique:
Keep the movement arm and body still in the contracted position for 2–3 seconds
Maximizes mechanical tension in the target musculature
Turnarounds When Muscles Unload Before Joint Limit
If the load begins to decrease before reaching full joint extension or contraction:
Initiate the turnaround immediately to maintain tension
Examples include lateral raises where shoulder muscles begin unloading or squats where quadriceps are unloaded prior to full extension
Core Principle Across All Turnarounds
Lower turnarounds must be smooth and continuous in all cases
Upper turnarounds are generally also continuous unless a controlled pause or squeeze technique is encouraged
Proper turnaround execution is arguably the single most important concept in the MSF Continuous Tension methodology (along with the gradual load up)
Summary
The turnaround technique governs the transitions between positive and negative phases of every repetition, ensuring continuous mechanical tension, joint safety, and optimal muscle loading. Proper execution requires attentiveness to equipment mechanics, joint and muscular limits, and the trainee’s range of motion. Mastery of smooth, continuous lower and upper turnarounds, along with the judicious use of pauses or squeeze techniques, is essential to delivering the full benefits of the MSF Continuous Tension protocol.
Set Duration (Number of Repetitions)
Typical Repetition Range
In the MSF methodology, the goal is to perform approximately 6 to 10 repetitions per set before reaching concentric failure. This range is generally appropriate for most intermediate and advanced clients and ensures that mechanical tension is applied at a stimulative level while maintaining control and proper form.
New exercises or novice trainees: 8 to 15 repetitions are often optimal
Lighter loads and higher repetitions allow for focus on learning technique
Provides opportunity to internalize MSF principles such as gradual load up, smooth turnarounds, and continuous tension
Load Selection Guidance
Repetitions fewer than five for intermediate or advanced clients may indicate that the weight is too heavy
Repetitions exceeding 10 may suggest the weight is too light
These are guidelines, not rigid rules, and should be applied in the context of each client’s ability and control
Load selection should prioritize:
Muscle tension quality over absolute load
Client control and safety
Gradual progression only when mastery of technique allows
Individual Variability
Set duration may vary depending on several factors:
Muscle fiber type predominance: Clients with higher proportions of fast-twitch fibers may fatigue faster
Psychological profile: Some clients may resist discomfort or intense effort
Afferent feedback: Muscle sensations such as burning or extreme fatigue may influence tolerance
Clients who initially respond better to shorter sets may need gradual progression and coaching to tolerate higher-intensity work. Those who benefit from longer sets may require slower increases in load to maintain proper form and technique.
Continuous Repetition Execution
All repetitions should be performed continuously, maintaining mechanical tension and avoiding unloading of the target muscles.
Stop a set only when the client is unable to continue positive movement with proper form
Achieving this point ensures full motor unit recruitment and a meaningful training stimulus
Continuous focus on quality over quantity reinforces the principles of the MSF Continuous Tension protocol
Summary
Set duration in MSF training is guided by client ability, exercise proficiency, and load selection, with 6–10 repetitions generally recommended for intermediate and advanced clients. Longer sets are appropriate for learning new exercises, while shorter sets may be required for fatigue-prone or intensity-adverse clients. All repetitions should be continuous, maintaining mechanical tension until the point where proper movement can no longer be sustained, ensuring both safety and maximal training stimulus.
Rest Intervals Between Exercises (…Not What We Were Led to Believe)
Misconceptions About Minimal Rest
A common belief in high-intensity training (HIT) is that workouts should minimize rest between exercises to maximize fatigue and, theoretically, hypertrophy. This approach is based on a misunderstanding:
Fatigue itself is not a stimulus for strength or hypertrophy; it actually limits the effectiveness of the workout
Rushing through exercises reduces the ability to fully recruit motor units, which are essential for producing mechanical tension and meaningful strength or hypertrophy gains
Limiting rest for safety reasons (by using lighter loads) may appear protective but reduces the stimulative potential of each exercise
Motor Unit Recruitment and Physiological Limits
Effective training requires maximal activation of motor units:
The nervous system naturally reduces the signal from the brain to muscles under fatigue to protect the body
If motor units are not recruited due to fatigue, their fibers cannot generate meaningful tension
Observing proper recovery ensures that clients can achieve their highest output on each exercise, maximizing the stimulus
Appropriate Intra-Workout Recovery
Rest between exercises should primarily allow for cardiorespiratory recovery, enabling clients to regain sufficient breathing control:
Typically, this takes 60–120 seconds, depending on the client and exercise intensity
Full recovery is not necessary; partial recovery allows for gradual improvement in fatigue resistance over time
Rest intervals are guided by observation and instructor judgment, not strict timing
Exercise Selection and Sequence Considerations
The type and order of exercises influence rest needs:
Larger compound movements (e.g., the Big 5) require more recovery between exercises
Smaller or isolated exercises (e.g., biceps curl following adduction) require less recovery
Overlapping movements or pre-fatigue pairings (e.g., chest flye into chest press) reduce performance in the second exercise and should be used selectively
Intelligent sequencing and spacing of exercises allows for higher quality performance and a more effective training stimulus
Instructor Application
Trainers should:
Avoid rushing from one exercise to the next
Prioritize maximal output for each exercise rather than minimal downtime
Adjust rest based on exercise intensity, client fatigue, and feedback
Discuss observations and adjustments with the team to continually improve programming (Kaizen)
Summary
Rest intervals between exercises are a critical component of effective high intensity resistance training. Fatigue is not a substitute for mechanical tension; inadequate recovery reduces motor unit recruitment and diminishes the stimulus for strength and hypertrophy. Appropriate intra-workout recovery allows clients to maintain high output, supports physiological adaptation, and ensures safe and effective exercise performance. Intelligent sequencing, observation, and adjustment of rest intervals are key responsibilities of the Exercise Practitioner.
Number of Sets
Single-Set Principle
In the MSF methodology, most exercises are performed with only one set. Research consistently shows that for the majority of clients, a single set taken to or near muscular failure provides a sufficient stimulus for strength and hypertrophy.
This approach emphasizes quality over quantity
The set must be performed with maximum focus and intensity to ensure all target muscle fibers are recruited and mechanical tension is achieved
One highly effective set allows for optimal recovery between workouts while minimizing unnecessary fatigue
Exceptions to the Single-Set Rule
Certain situations may warrant more than one set:
Rehabilitation clients or those who are severely deconditioned
Multiple sets may be performed at sub-maximal effort to safely build strength and tolerance
Intensity-adverse clients
Additional sets may help acclimate them to the demands of proper resistance training
Experienced clients having an “off day”
A second set can be performed after adequate rest if focus or effort was temporarily compromised.  Let the client catch their breath before going again.
Weight may be reduced to ensure proper form and control
This should be rare and not habitual, avoiding reliance on additional sets as a crutch
Instructor Responsibility
The MSF Exercise Practitioner must:
Ensure the client maximizes effort and focus during the primary set
Monitor for signs of inadequate intensity or fatigue
Apply additional sets or rest-pause techniques only when appropriate
Maintain the integrity of the training protocol, avoiding unnecessary deviation
Summary
For most clients, one set per exercise is sufficient when performed to or near muscular failure, highlighting the importance of focus, intensity, and quality. Multiple sets are reserved for rehabilitation, intensity-adverse clients, or rare lapses in effort, and must be applied judiciously to maintain the effectiveness and safety of the MSF training protocol.
Warm-up Considerations
General Approach
In MSF training, a separate warm-up set is generally unnecessary. The first several repetitions of an exercise performed at an appropriate resistance provide all the warm-up required.
Initial repetitions prime the target muscles for safe maximal effort later in the set
Fatiguing the muscle slightly reduces its force output, lowering the risk of injury to tendons and ligaments while still allowing for effective mechanical tension
Proper behavior and execution throughout the exercise are essential for this approach to remain safe
Mechanical and Physiological Benefits
Performing the exercise itself as a warm-up provides additional advantages:
Encourages fluid movement within the muscles and joints, improving lubrication
Prepares the tissues for full range of motion and controlled effort
Reduces the need for unnecessary additional sets or exercises that do not contribute directly to the training stimulus
Exceptions
Certain clients may require modified warm-up strategies:
Individuals with joint debilities or recent injuries may benefit from a lighter preparatory exercise to safely engage the joint
Example: Leg curl preceding leg press for a client recovering from knee replacement
Summary
MSF warm-up philosophy emphasizes efficiency and safety: performing the exercise itself at proper resistance provides all necessary preparation. Exceptions are reserved for clients with joint limitations, where a lighter preparatory movement may be required. The goal is to maximize readiness while minimizing unnecessary effort or risk.
