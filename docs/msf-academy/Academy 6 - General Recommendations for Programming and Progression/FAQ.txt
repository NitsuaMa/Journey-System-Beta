Higher reps with lighter weight vs. lower reps with heavier
weight?
The short answer…? You will end up at the same place if you are training to or
at least near failure, so from a high level perspective, it does not matter. But
here's what does matter:
Higher Reps (approx 10 plus) create a higher level of perceived effort through:
● More reps = more fatigue
● Increased cardiovascular demand
● Increased afferent feedback (discomfort, lactic acid, "burn")
● Longer duration, more mentally taxing
Perceived effort limits the level of motor unit recruitment that can be
achieved in any maximal effort contraction. Simply stated, the more we "feel"
any discomfort, the fewer muscle fibers we will be able to
activate/recruit/use. This is known as the maximum level of tolerable
effort...we just can't tolerate any more. And this likely happens well before we
get the required stimulus we seek from training (mechanical tension).
So this would lead us to believe that heavier weight and fewer reps are
superior. The problem with this approach is that most people are not capable
of handling a "heavier" weight that brings them to failure in 5 to 7 reps. They
don't have the ability to control that amount of load and they require more
"practice" with lighter loads and higher reps.
Usually with newer clients, we will automatically default to lighter weights for
purposes of learning all of the qualitative elements we require (posture, pace,
path) and particularly the lower turnarounds. Over time, we begin to increase
the loads and their reps should come down, but only to the extent that they
maintain their form. Oftentimes we'll reach a point where the client begins to
wrestle the weight rather than use it with control. We need to constantly
remind them that there is a standard of quality to maintain. But if I have a
client that is compliant and follows instruction, I will absolutely try and
progress them to a lower rep range.
Am I getting stronger?
Aside from asking “was that more weight?” asking “am I getting stronger?”
seems to be one of the more frequently asked questions. And while we could
simply answer yes or no…they would most likely require further explanation to
satisfy them. With this being the case, a little background information is
necessary for perspective.
Firstly, anyone engaging in a properly designed, safely performed,
progressively challenged strength training program can expect to get
stronger. This is especially true for those that haven't done any sort of
strength training in the recent past. So most assuredly we can answer “yes”
to that inquiry. But the reason they are asking must be because they are
unaware themselves or need to be convinced. This is an unfortunate
occurrence.
It could be that the person asking lives a relatively inactive life, in which case
they would not have an opportunity to notice or prove to themselves that they
have in fact gotten stronger. Again, this is unfortunate but probably not that
uncommon. But if this is the case, then any amount of strength training done
with any level of intensity should represent a profound “wake up call” of sorts.
Or it could be that this particular individual is just physically unaware of
themselves, which would tend to be the case for a good number of our clients
as that is what possibly drew them to MSF in the first place. In other words,
they don’t know how to tell or measure their new levels of strength and want
an objective measurement from the workout chart. While the answer would
be, “yes, you are lifting more weight than you did when you started,” that can
be a little misleading.
It can almost be guaranteed that one will go on to lift more weight at MSF
than when they first joined. Part of the reason is because we will start
conservatively with them, using significantly lighter weights than they are
currently capable of lifting to facilitate the learning process. It takes time and
attention to learn how to properly strength train and our focus is always on
the qualitative approach. So the relative light weights that we start with will
soon be surpassed as the client learns how to control their movement and
effort. In this case, it is likely that they have gotten stronger as the
neurological adaptations alone that take place very quickly, on a rep-to-rep
basis at times, are a big component of getting stronger. On a side note,
strength itself is not an adaptation, but rather an outcome. There are 6 or
more adaptations that go into making someone stronger of which
neurological efficiency and muscle mass are two of the major ones.
Now in the case of an advanced client that has been training for 3 - 5 years or
more, there is a chance that they have reached their genetic “ceiling” in terms
of strength. In fact everyone is likely to reach this point if they subject
themselves to enough stimulus (i.e. training sessions). But once we reach this
point, it would make zero sense to stop training as we would then begin to
lose our strength. So it’s best to just keep training without concern for a
genetic limit. Just assume that it doesn’t even exist, keep training, and at
least maintain as much strength as you can for the rest of your life.
All of that to say that the real bottleneck in all of this usually comes down to
the willingness or tolerance of the client to produce a truly hard and adaptive
effort. When someone first begins a strength training program (or any
physical activity for that matter), almost everything will be stimulative as it will
be at a level above what they are currently used to. However, as one gets
stronger, the current level of effort will not be enough to surpass the newfound
level of strength. A harder, more intense effort is required to keep progressing.
This is a simple biological adaptation model. The more someone has
engaged in training, the higher the level of output is required to be
stimulative. But the good news is, with more training comes more ability and
tolerance and skill. The subject should be capable of training harder because
of the practice of training and the instruction they receive. This is the primary
purpose of an Exercise Practitioner. Aside from ensuring their safety of course.
There should be no doubt to the subject that they themselves have gotten
stronger if they are consistently training with us and they are focused on their
intensity and technique. They should be able to feel the difference proper
strength training makes by just standing there with an awareness. If not and
we need to point to other examples, we have gotten plenty of input over the
years:
● Posture
● Less joint pain
● Getting out of bed
● Up and down stairs
● Getting in and out of the car
● Golfing
● Carrying groceries
● Lifting up children
● Yardwork
● Opening doors
● Walking through stores or the neighborhood
● Loading/unloading items from the trunk
● Getting off the floor
● Getting out of a chair
● Lifting suitcase into overhead storage
All of these would be indicative of an increase in strength. They are all also
improved with practice as any physical endeavor is specific. If one wants to
get better at going up and down stairs or getting off the floor, these should be
carefully practiced. The strength increase will make all of those things easier
but the point being that there is also a skill component.
So to sum up, yes, everyone that begins training with us will assuredly
become stronger. The question is, how much and to what degree? And this is
entirely up to the individual. How seriously will they take their sessions and the
instruction? How much will they challenge themselves to get better at
strength training? The Instructor will not hold them back and they will even
encourage, but ultimately it is up to the client to decide how hard they want
to work over time. In this way, they will not ever have to question whether they
are getting stronger, they will give their body no choice.

Osteoporosis, Osteopenia, and Bone Density Concerns
Is strength training safe if I have osteoporosis or osteopenia?
Yes, when it is done correctly and under proper supervision. At MaxStrength
Fitness, exercises are controlled, customized to your abilities, and progressed
gradually. This allows your muscles and bones to adapt safely without
unnecessary strain.
Can strength training actually improve bone density?
Strength training can help slow bone loss and, in many cases, improve bone
density. When muscles work against resistance, they pull on bones and signal
the body to maintain or build stronger bone tissue. This type of effort is
especially important for bone health. Additionally, high-intensity strength
training encourages the release of chemical messengers in the body known
as myokines that signal the creation of bone tissue.
Will lifting weights increase my risk of fractures?
Our Continuous Tension protocol and supervision ensure that the strength
training is performed safely and avoids excessive or sudden loading during
exercise. Properly performed strength training reduces fracture risk over time.
Stronger muscles support and protect your bones, improve joint stability, and
lower the risk of falls, which is the most common cause of fractures in people
with low bone density.
What if I already have joint pain or past injuries?
Exercises are always adjusted to your comfort level and medical history.
Machines, ranges of motion, and resistance levels are selected specifically to
avoid painful or unsafe positions while still providing benefit.
Do I need to lift heavy weights for bone health?
You need enough resistance to challenge your muscles, but “heavy” is relative
and always individualized. The goal is meaningful effort with precision control,
not lifting maximal weights.
How long does it take to see benefits?
Improvements in strength, balance, and confidence often occur within a few
sessions. Changes in bone density take longer to physiologically manifest and
require consistency, but every workout contributes to protecting your
long-term bone health.
Why not just do walking or light exercise?
Walking is excellent for overall health, but it does not provide enough stimulus
to meaningfully strengthen bones. Strength training applies the kind of
external resistance bones need to adapt and stay strong.
What makes MaxStrength Fitness different?
Our training focuses on slow, controlled movement, individualized resistance,
and focused supervision. This allows us to safely work with clients who may
not be comfortable with traditional gyms.
