How Intensely to Push a Client
A framework for instructor exploration in finding the all important measure of a client's tolerance of effort.
1. What Are We Actually Trying to Achieve During the Workout?
Purpose of Resistance Training
Create a stimulus that produces strength, hypertrophy, and positive health adaptations.
The stimulus occurs when high mechanical tension is experienced by muscle fibers.
Mechanical tension is maximized when:
Effort is high
Motor unit recruitment is high
Contraction speed (i.e. movement arm) slows involuntarily
These happen closer to task failure
Lifting light weights for higher reps and creating a “burn” is not stimulative
Muscular Failure myth
“Muscular Failure” is not a structural phenomenon.  Our muscles do not fail under load.  This would only happen in an injurious scenario.
It is more accurately termed task failure — the point where the subject reaches their maximum tolerable perception of effort and decides to stop the task.
This point is highly individualized.
This point is also able to increase over time with practice.  In other words, the best way to learn to tolerate training is by training; but it has to be intentional and not simply going through the motions!
2. What Does Appropriate Intensity Look Like (Eventually)?
A set is appropriately intense when:
The movement arm begins to involuntarily slow (“bogging down”).
The client must voluntarily increase effort to continue moving.
Concentric speed cannot be maintained without greater volitional effort. This increased effort creates higher levels of tension!
Quality (posture, pace, path, gradual load-up, continuous tension, controlled turnarounds) must be maintained.
Beginners/novice trainees may not need involuntary slowing for training to be productive.  Quality execution should be the focus.
This combination ensures:
High motor unit recruitment (MUR)
High mechanical tension
Minimal unnecessary fatigue
Safety is the priority
3. The Instructor’s Role: Calibrating Intensity
Intensity is not forced — it is observed, discussed, and progressed.
A. Observational Cues
Monitor:
Facial expression (calm vs. strained)
Verbal feedback during set and in between
Breathing/oxygen debt
Body language (stillness vs. shifting)
Ability to maintain posture, pace, and path
B. Direct Communication
Ask early in a set (for a new/intensity adverse client):
“Does this weight feel reasonable?”
“Is this challenging in the way you expected?”
C. Instructor Responsibility
If effort is:
Too low - Increase load or reinforce qualitative execution.
Too high - Reduce load, reduce reps, or refine execution.
Misaligned with expectation - Discuss and recalibrate.
Always reassure:
Dialing in intensity is a skill that is individual specific and is critical to the development of a master level trainer. It is the instructor's responsibility!
If effort was perceived as too high, discuss with the subject and reassure them that we will adjust going forward. Empower them by reminding them that their effort is always under their control, we are simply facilitators.  Unfortunately this is not an exact science and there is a learning curve for both but their safety is our highest priority.
If effort was perceived as too low, reassure the subject that they are still in the learning phase and that quality execution is the priority.  We will also speed up the learning curve for them by adding more resistance to ensure an appropriate challenge going forward but quality comes first!
As noted above, in either case the instructor should determine the appropriateness of the load and make a change mid-set if deemed necessary. It is ultimately the instructor’s responsibility to deliver a remarkable client experience. Do not waste their time or money with anything less.
Both trainer and subject improve with practice.
4. Quality Before Intensity
Execution of the Continuous Tension protocol should be continually improved; rep to rep and workout to workout.  The instructor must provide reminders and hold them accountable.
Controlled turnarounds are non-negotiable.
Gradual load-up for applicable exercises is equally important.
Less than “perfect” execution acts to artificially reduce perceived effort. It also unloads the targeted musculature.
If a set “felt easy,” evaluate:
Was the initial load up gradual?
Were turnarounds exaggerated and controlled?
Was pace/tension continuous?
Never sacrifice quality to just “keep the machine moving”.
5. Rep Range Framework
There is no single magic rep number.
The 6–10 range exists for structure and consistency of application.
General Guidelines
< 6 reps → Load may be too heavy. Can feel oppressive.
6–10 reps → Typically appropriate challenge.
> 10 reps → Load may be too light. Creates more discomfort.
~15 reps → Practical upper limit due to duration and focus.
Important Exceptions
Advanced subjects:
4–5 reps can be appropriate.
Heavier loads will produce strong stimulus sooner with less fatigue.
Newer but active subjects:
10–12 reps may accelerate learning through more “practice”.
If 15+ reps are possible with proper quality:
End the set.
Increase weight next session.
Emphasize the importance of every rep being “practice”.
Remember:
Lighter weights + higher reps = more discomfort/fatigue.
Fatigue is not the goal.
Mechanical tension from high effort is the goal.
6. Special Populations & Intensity Sensitivity
A. True Novices
Do not require maximal effort for stimulus.
Moderate load + skill acquisition can be sufficient.
Focus on control and learning the details (4 P’s).
B. Intensity-Adverse / Deconditioned
Easily overwhelmed.
Require lighter loads AND controlled rep counts.
Avoid excessive reps.
Keep upper limit ~15 reps (often fewer).
Build trust through careful progression.
Load alone does not usually cause the adverse response —
Total stress exposure does.
7. High-Drive/Experienced Clients
These clients:
Expect a challenge from day one.
Have higher tolerable perception of effort.
May disengage if under-challenged.
Instructor actions:
Avoid wasting time with light loads.
Assess load within first few reps.
Pause and increase weight if clearly too light.
Ensure sets approach meaningful (i.e. involuntary) slowing.
Give them:
Adequate challenge
Efficient stimulus
Instruction to maintain quality while increasing effort
8. When to Stop a Set
Stop when:
Ideally - Concentric failure (i.e. task failure) is reached with maintained quality.  Basically, the loss of range of motion on the concentric.
Practically - Rep count reaches practical upper limit (~ 12-15).
Technique degrades.
Client is overwhelmed.
Safety is compromised.
You should:
Pause mid-set to adjust load.
End a set early to protect quality. If subject has clearly lost their form, they are no longer training, they are surviving.
Provide immediate corrective instruction.  Even better would be to proactively provide instruction to prevent discrepancies.
9. Hierarchy of Priorities
Safety
Quality of execution
Appropriate effort (near task failure)
Established rep framework
The instructor is ultimately responsible for:
The safety of the client
The intensity of the session
The client’s experience
The effectiveness of the stimulus
10. How to End a Set Based on Subject and Exercise
Ideally, most clients will reach concentric failure somewhere between 6 and 10 reps, sometimes up to 12 if necessary.  For the intensity adverse or deconditioned however, this may not be advisable as they tend to be more sensitive to loading and fatigue.
Most clients should reach concentric failure between 6–10 reps (occasionally up to 12).
Concentric failure = loss of full range of motion.
Only full reps are recorded; reps after this point are not counted for purposes of charting and progressing.
Partial Reps After Concentric Failure
Sets may continue past concentric failure using partial reps to increase muscular tension and stimulus.
This would only be for the clients that are deemed capable and tolerant of this level of increased intensity.
Most exercises lend themselves to partial reps, but the following do not:
Leg Press
Chest Press
Overhead Press
Seated Dip
Cervical
Lumbar
Partial reps (i.e. reps beyond concentric failure) are NOT advised for compound pushing movements (LP, CP, OH, SD):
Partial reps are often not possible once failure is reached due to inability to produce further concentric movement.  The subject will most likely get “stuck” at the lower turn after reaching concentric failure on the preceding rep.
Partial reps (i.e. reps beyond concentric failure) are NOT advised for Cervical and Lumbar:
Safety concerns attributable to the delicate structures of the spine and nerves.
Concentric failure is also not recommended.  Subjects should be capable of a full range of motion for all reps attempted.
Use an abundance of caution in both client selection and training intensity.
These two movements may not be advisable for all clients.
Considerations for Intensity-Adverse Clients
May not be appropriate for:
Concentric failure
Partial reps
Instructor must use judgment to avoid adverse responses.
As an alternative, consider an isometric intensifier to finish a set when concentric failure has not been reached.
Isometric Intensifier to End a Set (Primary Strategy for Intensity-Adverse Clients)
Purpose
Attempt to create meaningful effort and stimulus without requiring concentric failure.
General Application
Performed after the last full rep (before failure is reached).
Implemented at a specific point in the range of motion.
Typically lasts ~ 5 to 10 seconds with instructor-guided countdown and encouragement.
See next section below for specific application.
Isometric Intensifier - Application by Exercise Type
1. Compound Pushing Movements (LP, CP, OH, SD)
Perform isometric hold during the final eccentric phase.
Ideal position:
Near midpoint of range of motion
Slightly below midpoint (i.e. closer to lower turn) for increased intensity
Execution:
Client performs a slow “final descent”
Instructor cues a pause at a challenging but controllable position
Client works against the load to prevent movement
Safety feature:
Occurs during eccentric on the way to the lower turn where the load will be set down
Key Benefits
Increases effort without requiring failure
Client feels more capable and in control
Reduces likelihood of quitting early
2. All Other Movements (excluding Cervical/Lumbar)
Perform isometric hold at or near the contracted position.
Execution:
After last full concentric, hold the contracted position
Maintain tension with no movement
Instructor provides countdown and encouragement
Key Benefits
Occurs at a familiar and achievable position
Encourages engagement and effort
Clear “finish line” improves compliance
3. Cervical and Lumbar
Do NOT train to concentric failure.
Do NOT use partial reps.
Use only cautious isometric holds at the contracted position for appropriate subjects.
Coaching Component (Critical Across All Applications)
Instructor must:
Provide continuous encouragement
Count down remaining time (e.g., “5 more, 4 more…”)
Clients need:
A clear endpoint (“finish line”)
Ongoing verbal support to maintain effort
Key Takeaway
For intensity-adverse clients, isometric intensifiers are the most effective way to increase effort and stimulus while maintaining safety, confidence, and engagement—without requiring concentric failure.
Importantly, these isometric intensifiers are only used at the END of a set, never at random points prior to the end.
Important for New Instructors
Every client has a unique maximum tolerable perception of effort.  Your job is to find it — safely and systematically.
A subject’s maximum tolerable perception of effort will improve with exposure to more training! This is the long-term “secret weapon”.
The instructor is responsible for ensuring that a stimulus has been created.
Most clients should eventually approach task failure in 6–10 reps.  This can easily take months to arrive at.
Some never will — and that’s acceptable. We need to be creative and progress more slowly with these subjects.
Quality always precedes intensity.
Mechanical tension with high effort is the stimulus.
Fatigue, soreness, burning, sensations, etc. are NOT the objective and more importantly are NOT stimulative.
