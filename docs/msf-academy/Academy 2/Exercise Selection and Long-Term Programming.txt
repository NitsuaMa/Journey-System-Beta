Exercise Selection and Long-Term Programming
When deciding whether to add an exercise to a client’s program, the Exercise Practitioner must evaluate several factors. The goal is not simply to increase the number of exercises performed, but to ensure that every addition improves the client’s results and experience without compromising the quality or intensity of the workout.
The following framework should guide the decision making process.
Key Questions Before Adding an Exercise
Will the addition improve or maintain the appropriate training frequency for the targeted muscles?
Will the exercise provide sufficient overlap in the muscle fibers being trained if different exercises are used between workouts?
Does the addition address a goal or concern expressed by the client, either verbally or through their intake information (demographics)?
Is there sufficient time/room available in the workout to perform the exercise with proper control and execution?
Can the client maintain the necessary intensity of effort if another exercise is added?
Will the addition improve the overall client experience without compromising training quality?
Each of these factors should be considered carefully before expanding a program. Keep in mind that most clients typically perform at least 6 exercises per workout (after the initial “learning curve” period of around 4 to 6 workouts).  More advanced subjects might be able to perform 8 exercises or possibly more in a session.  The points outlined below will help determine how to calibrate session volume on a client by client basis.
1. Training Frequency
A twice per week stimulus is necessary to optimize long term muscular adaptations.
Strength itself is not an adaptation but rather the outcome of a multitude of underlying adaptations, many of which are neurological. Because of this, strength can often be built or maintained with a once per week stimulus to that specific movement pattern.
Muscle growth, however, requires more consistent stimulation. Some hypertrophy will occur initially with a once per week workout, but progress will typically plateau soon after the base level adaptations are achieved.
Training once per week is still beneficial. Strength can be maintained and sometimes improved, and some muscle can be built or maintained. However, if the goal is to optimize muscular development and long-term progress, a twice per week stimulus is the more effective approach.
When deciding whether to add an exercise, the practitioner should consider whether the addition will help ensure that the targeted muscle groups (and more specifically, muscle fibers) are being stimulated twice per week.
2. Fiber Specific Adaptation and Exercise Overlap
Hypertrophy occurs within individual muscle fibers that experience sufficient mechanical tension.
If a muscle fiber is not recruited and exposed to meaningful mechanical tension, it will not benefit from the workout.
This principle has important implications for program design. Only the muscle fibers that are recruited in both workouts of the week will receive the necessary frequency of stimulation for optimal progress.
If certain hip muscles, for example, are stimulated in only one workout but not the second, the progress of those muscles will not reach full potential.
For this reason, performing the exact same exercises in both workouts is often the most reliable way to ensure that the same fibers are being trained consistently.
However, clients often prefer some variation in their programming. When variation is introduced, the practitioner must ensure that there is still substantial overlap in the muscle groups we wish to emphasize.
An example would be performing a pulldown in one workout and performing a pullover combined with a biceps curl in the other workout. In this case, the latissimus dorsi and biceps are still being trained in both sessions.
The key point is that if exercises differ between workouts, there must still be meaningful overlap in the muscle fibers being trained.  And this becomes increasingly important as a subject becomes more advanced.
3. Client Feedback and Requests
Most clients will eventually express curiosity or interest in additional exercises or machines.
These requests may appear in several ways. A client may ask when they will be able to try other machines in the studio. They may express concern that a particular area of their body is not receiving enough attention. They may state, for instance, that they “feel their midsection needs more work”, or they may simply say that they would like to “do more machines”.
In these situations the Exercise Practitioner must be listening and ensure that the client feels heard.  They should also be prepared to teach and inform and teach the client the fundamentals of resistance training including exercise selection and programming.  The points made throughout this document and the entire MSF Academy are the considerations to be made for client education.
From the beginning of the client relationship, the practitioner should emphasize the importance of quality execution and effort rather than the number or variety of exercises performed. Clients should understand that results are driven by effort and mechanical tension applied to the muscles, not by the number of machines used during a workout. Importantly, the emphasis of quality over quantity is the primary attribute of the MSF protocol that helps to ensure the safety of each and every client.
At the same time, delivering a remarkable client experience is a priority. If a requested exercise can be added without compromising the quality or effectiveness of the workout, it is often appropriate to accommodate the request. Just expect that it will most likely have to become part of the routine moving forward in order to be effective and not just an occasional inclusion when requested.
The practitioner must balance education with responsiveness to the client’s interests.
4. Client Goals from the Demographics Packet
Exercise Practitioners should be very familiar with the information contained in the client’s demographics or intake form.
Clients frequently list goals such as strengthening the core, toning the arms, or improving overall strength. If a client takes the time to complete this form, it is important that the program clearly addresses the concerns they identified.
These goals should be discussed early in the client’s journey. The practitioner should explain which exercises in the program are intended to address those goals.
As discussed previously, targeted muscle groups should ideally be trained twice per week. In many cases this will mean performing the same exercise in both workouts in order to ensure consistent stimulation of the relevant muscle fibers.
Ensuring that client goals are clearly addressed in the exercise selection process can significantly improve client relationships and engagement.
5. Time Constraints Within the Workout
Every workout has a practical time limit.
When new clients begin training, the loads used are typically lighter in order to allow them to learn proper technique and develop control of the equipment.
Lighter loads generally lead to longer sets, often exceeding ten repetitions. Because of this, sets tend to take longer to complete and the total number of exercises that can be performed within the workout is limited.
During this stage of training the primary focus should be on precise execution and exaggerated control of the load.
It is important to remember that twenty minutes is the minimum amount of time for a workout, not the standard amount of time.  Instructors should not rush or eliminate exercises in an effort to complete the workout sooner (i.e. within 20 minutes).
As clients accumulate training experience they often become capable of producing the desired stimulus with heavier loads and fewer repetitions. As set durations shorten, additional exercises may become feasible within the available time.
This progression should be explained to clients early in their training experience. Practitioners should reinforce that quality execution is always the priority and that there may be opportunities to expand the program as the client becomes more advanced.
If a requested exercise can easily be added without compromising quality or time constraints, it may be appropriate to include it. However, the practitioner should not allow the program to be dictated entirely by the client’s desire to use more machines.
6. Intensity Limits Within the Workout
Just as every workout has a time limit, every workout should also have an intensity limit.
Advanced clients who train with precision and focus are capable of generating very high levels of effort. Because of this, there is a practical limit to the number of exercises that can be performed at the intensity required to create a meaningful stimulus.
Performing an additional exercise does not automatically lead to additional benefit. A certain level of intensity is required for an exercise to be productive.
There is an inverse relationship between intensity and volume. As the intensity of effort increases, the total amount of work that can be performed decreases.
For a highly focused and experienced client performing seven or eight exercises with appropriate intensity, it becomes increasingly unlikely that additional exercises can be added without reducing the quality of effort.
While there may be occasional exceptions, the instructor should carefully observe the client’s effort level and remind them that their focus should be on getting more out of the exercises they are already performing rather than simply adding more exercises/volume.
As training experience increases, the importance of intensity of effort becomes even greater to insure a stimulus.
7. Establishing Authority and Building Trust
Exercise Practitioners should position themselves as knowledgeable authorities in strength training, but this authority must be earned through trust.
Trust is built through listening carefully to clients and approaching each interaction with genuine interest and empathy.
Practitioners should listen first and then look to inform/educate.
If a client raises a question that the practitioner cannot immediately answer, it is appropriate to acknowledge the question and indicate that further research or discussion with colleagues will be conducted. The instructor should then follow up with the client as soon as possible with a well informed response.
Additionally, the usefulness and insightfulness of the practitioner’s instruction goes a long way in establishing credibility with the clients.  The MSF team should constantly strive to master their craft through reading, meetings, and discussion on various training topics. It is imperative in this profession that an instructor continue to stay engaged and add to their skill base.
This process strengthens trust and reinforces the professionalism of the MSF team.
When trust has been established, clients are more likely to accept guidance regarding the structure of their workouts, including decisions about whether additional exercises should or should not be included.
In some cases, a client may feel that a muscle group is not being addressed or that the workouts are “not challenging”. More often than not, the true issue is that the exercise is not being performed with enough intensity and/or proper technique. In these situations the solution will be improved execution rather than additional exercises and the practitioner must first be aware and then convincing enough to communicate this. Trust will only help the instructor in these situations.
Quality execution should always remain the priority.
Final Perspective
Every decision regarding exercise selection should support two goals. The first is delivering the most efficient, effective, and safe training stimulus possible. The second is providing a remarkable experience for every client during every workout.
Adding an exercise to a program is often the easiest decision to make. However, this should only occur after careful consideration of the factors outlined in this document.
Exercise Practitioners are not simply guiding clients through workouts. They are teaching a skill that can significantly influence long term health and physical capability.
Because of this, the practitioner must demonstrate attention to detail, thoughtful program design, and a clear commitment to the client’s success.
When this approach is consistently applied, clients begin to recognize that the practitioner cares more about the workout than they do. Over time this perspective helps shift the client’s focus away from quantity and toward the quality of effort and execution that ultimately drives progress.
